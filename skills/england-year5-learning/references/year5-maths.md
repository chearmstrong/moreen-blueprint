# Year 5 maths reference: England

Use the [Department for Education's Year 5 mathematics programme](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-mathematics-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-mathematics-programmes-of-study#year-5-programme-of-study) as the curriculum source. The curriculum expects pupils to move between representations, reason about their methods, and consolidate earlier understanding before moving on. Schools can choose when within a key stage to introduce content.

## Curriculum map

Year 5 includes place value to at least 1,000,000; addition and subtraction with numbers over four digits; multiplication, division, factors and multiples; fractions, decimals and percentages; metric measures, perimeter and area; shapes and angles; reflection and translation; and reading line graphs and tables. These are **curriculum topics**, not proof that the child's class has covered them. For a topic outside this map, consult the official programme rather than guessing its year.

Choose the representation that exposes the particular relationship:

| Topic | Useful representation | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Place value | Labelled columns with each digit's value | Can the child explain why 7 means 7,000 rather than 700? |
| Written calculation | Aligned columns or a partitioned number | Does the child understand a regrouped ten, rather than only reciting a carry rule? |
| Multiplication and division | Equal groups or an array | Can the child link groups, multiplication and division? Interpret a remainder in the story. |
| Fractions, decimals, percentages | Equal-area bar or number line | Are the wholes the same size? Can the child connect, for example, one half, 0.5 and 50%? |
| Measure and geometry | Labelled shape with units | Is the child counting boundary length for perimeter or square units for area? |
| Data | Clearly scaled table or graph | Can the child read the scale before comparing values? |

Offer a smaller number or simpler representation when the first method fails, then return to the original problem. A challenge should deepen the same idea, such as asking why two methods agree, rather than racing ahead to a Year 6 procedure.

## Rounding example

The programme includes rounding numbers up to 1,000,000 to the nearest 10, 100, 1,000, 10,000 and 100,000. Early in the year, begin with smaller examples when needed.

For rounding, show the two neighbouring multiples and the halfway value. Place the number on a correctly scaled number line, or use an ordered comparison that makes no claim about distance. For example:

```text
60,000 < 65,000 (halfway) < 67,210 < 70,000
```

That example rounds to 70,000 to the nearest 10,000 because 67,210 is above halfway. This comparison shows order, not distance. If drawing a number line instead, position 67,210 roughly 72% of the way from 60,000 to 70,000. If the halfway value is exactly met, use the usual school convention of rounding up and say so.

## When a first explanation fails

**Regrouping, stuck:** If the child says, “I cannot do 402 − 178 because there are no tens to borrow”, do not just say “borrow from the hundreds”. Show that 402 is 4 hundreds, 0 tens and 2 ones, and can also be 3 hundreds, 9 tens and 12 ones. Keep columns aligned and ask only, “What is 12 − 8?” before continuing. If the child still cannot see where the 9 tens came from, try 42 − 18 with tens and ones, then return to 402 − 178. The school's written layout may differ; keep the place-value relationship while following it.

**Fractions, correct but unexplained:** If the child says 1/3 is larger than 1/4 without a reason, draw two equal-length bars, split one into three equal parts and the other into four, and shade one part of each. Ask which shaded part is wider and why the whole bars must match. A correct guess alone is not a signal to increase difficulty.

A visual is a temporary support, not an end in itself. Treat this note as teaching guidance, not a substitute for the child's teacher or school's sequence.
